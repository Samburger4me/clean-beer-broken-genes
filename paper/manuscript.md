# 1. Title and Abstract {.unnumbered}

::: titleblock
[Clean Beer, Broken Genes]{.ptitle}

[Tracing how brewing yeasts lost the clove flavor]{.psub}

[Sam Sperling^1^, Alissa Subillaga^1^ and Christian Lucio^1^]{.pauth}

[^1^The University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX 78712, USA · BCH 339N Bioinformatics, Fall 2026]{.paffil}
:::

::: abstract
Brewing yeasts are strains of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* that people have selected for centuries for how they ferment and how the beer tastes. One taste most brewers avoid is the clove-like phenolic off-flavor (POF), 4-vinylguaiacol (4-VG). Yeast needs two enzymes to make it, Pad1 and Fdc1 [@mukai2010; @payne2015; @white2015], and many beer yeasts have lost it [@mukai2014; @gallone2016]. We hypothesized that beer lineages lost POF several separate times, through different loss-of-function (LOF) mutations, rather than inheriting one loss from a single ancestor. Using public genome sequences, a gene tree and a test that corrects for how closely strains are related, we find {{R['a2_n_distinct_lof']}} distinct LOF mutations that add up to at least {{R['a2_n_loss_events']}} separate losses of POF, {{R['a2_n_beverage_loss_events']}} of them in beverage yeasts. Across {{R['core_n_strains']}} strains, {{pct(R['core_fisher_domestic']['a_POFneg'], R['core_fisher_domestic']['a_POFneg']+R['core_fisher_domestic']['a_POFpos'])}} domesticated beverage strains carry a predicted loss-of-function (LOF) genotype, compared with {{pct(R['core_fisher_domestic']['b_POFneg'], R['core_fisher_domestic']['b_POFneg']+R['core_fisher_domestic']['b_POFpos'])}} wild, wine and bioethanol strains, and the difference holds in most groups of related strains (Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio {{f1(R['a1_cmh_or'])}}, {{P(R['a1_cmh_p'])}}). The common *FDC1* mutations cut the enzyme short before every residue that binds its cofactor or metal or does the chemistry. Our DNA-based calls predict the laboratory-measured phenotype with {{pc(R['a3_sensitivity'][0])}} sensitivity and {{pc(R['a3_specificity'][0])}} specificity. Maltose-transporter genes tell a different story: beer strains traded copies of *MPH2/3* for *MAL31* rather than gaining copies overall. Removing a flavor by repeated, independent gene loss is the mirror image of today's efforts to add flavors to brewing yeast.
:::

Humans domesticated *S. cerevisiae* long before they knew it existed. Genome surveys show that industrial strains differ from wild ones and fall into a few domesticated families, or lineages, each shaped by the drink it ferments [@fay2005; @liti2009; @gallone2016; @goncalves2016; @peter2018; @duan2018]. Beer yeasts are the clearest case. Gallone *et al.* sequenced and tested 157 industrial strains and found beer strains concentrated in two lineages (Beer 1 and Beer 2) that show signs of human selection on sugar use and aroma [@gallone2016; @gallone2018].

Aroma is the trait brewers select most directly, because they taste every batch. One aroma, the clove-like 4-vinylguaiacol (4-VG), is wanted in wheat beers but counts as an off-flavor in most other styles [@coghe2004]. Yeast makes 4-VG from ferulic acid, which malt releases during mashing, by cutting off its carboxyl group [@coghe2004]. Two neighboring genes on chromosome IV do this. *FDC1* encodes the decarboxylase, and *PAD1* encodes the enzyme that makes its prenylated-FMN (prFMN) cofactor [@mukai2010; @payne2015; @white2015] (Fig. 1a). Breaking either gene therefore gives the same clove-free, "POF−" phenotype. Earlier surveys found premature stop codons (nonsense mutations) and frameshifts in both genes in ale, lager, saké and shochu yeasts [@mukai2014; @gallone2016], and one *FDC1* stop codon (Q154\*) in most POF− strains of the Beer 1 lineage [@mertens2019].

Three questions remain open. First, many beer strains are close relatives, so simply counting strains counts one old mutation many times. Second, nobody has counted how many separate times POF was lost, which is what tells us whether brewers selected against it again and again. Third, predictions of POF from DNA sequence have rarely been checked against laboratory measurements. We hypothesized that beer lineages carry several distinct, lineage-specific LOF mutations in *PAD1* and *FDC1* that set them apart from wild strains even after accounting for relatedness. To test this, we combined public genome sequences, a family tree (phylogeny) of *PAD1*–*FDC1* sequences, a test stratified by population group (clade), laboratory phenotypes and the Fdc1 crystal structure (Fig. 1b). As a contrast, we also ask whether a second domestication trait, maltose-transporter copy number, changed in the same lineages.

# 2. Data Sources and Computational Methods

**Sequence data.** We took the *S. cerevisiae* S288C reference coding sequences (CDS) of *PAD1* and *FDC1* from NCBI RefSeq NC_001136.10 and those of *MAL31*, *MAL11*, *MPH2* and *MPH3* from NC_001134.8, NC_001139.9, NC_001136.10 and NC_001142.9 [@sayers2025; @wong2023]. Allele records came from two sources. (i) A megablast search of each CDS against NCBI core_nt, restricted to *S. cerevisiae* (txid4932) and *S. pastorianus* (txid27292) (E ≤ 10^−50^, URL API) [@altschul1990; @camacho2009]. This found the Mukai *et al.* strain panel [@mukai2014] and several complete genomes. (ii) Chromosome IV of the 157 genomes from Gallone *et al.* [@gallone2016] (BioProject PRJNA323691, records CM004674.1–CM007170.1). These records are in nuccore but not in core_nt, so we located each CDS by anchored k-mer search followed by global alignment. In total we analyzed {{R['core_n_records']['PAD1']}} *PAD1* and {{R['core_n_records']['FDC1']}} *FDC1* records from {{R['core_n_strains']}} strains. Strain clade labels and POF phenotypes came from Gallone *et al.* Tables S1 and S5 [@gallone2016]. Copy-number data came from the 1,011 Yeast Genomes project [@peter2018]: the read-depth gene copy-number matrix (1002genomes.u-strasbg.fr) and Supplementary Table 1 (ecological origin and clade). The structure was PDB 4ZAC (Fdc1 with prFMN and Mn^2+^) [@berman2000; @payne2015]; Pad1 was the AlphaFold model AF-P33751-F1 [@jumper2021; @varadi2024]. Features came from UniProt Q03034 and P33751 and Pfam via InterPro [@uniprot2025; @blum2025; @mistry2021]. Every download is listed with its URL, date and SHA-256 checksum in `data/MANIFEST.csv`.

**Troubleshooting that shaped the pipeline.** Four problems changed the design. (1) The project proposal cited BioProject PRJNA323654 for the Gallone genomes; that accession belongs to an unrelated project, and the correct one is PRJNA323691. (2) NCBI requests sometimes failed or timed out, so every acquisition step is cache-first: it reads a local file if present and queries NCBI only otherwise. A whole-genome BLAST restricted to PRJNA323691 did not finish in about 30 min, which is why the Gallone loci were located by direct alignment instead. (3) Two *FDC1* stop codons (W497\*, Y500\*) remove at most {{late_removed()}} of the protein. We did not call these LOF by default (rule below) and then tested that choice against the phenotype. (4) Our first copy-number test counted *MAL/MPH* loci in chromosome-level assemblies (n = {{R['core_mal_assembly'][3]['domesticated_n']}} domesticated vs {{R['core_mal_assembly'][3]['wild_lab_n']}} wild/laboratory). Assemblies collapse near-identical subtelomeric repeats, so we replaced it with read-depth copy number from 1,011 genomes.

**Variant and LOF calling.** Each allele was aligned to the S288C CDS with Biopython `PairwiseAligner` [@cock2009] (global; match 2, mismatch −3, gap open −10, extend −1), following the class notebooks. Protein translations were aligned with BLOSUM62 (open −11, extend −1) [@needleman1970; @henikoff1992]. Indels were right-shifted to HGVS positions. An allele was called LOF if it had a premature stop codon or a frameshift that ends the protein before 95% of the reference length. A strain was predicted POF− if all of its callable records for either gene were LOF, POF+ if both genes were callable and intact, and unresolved otherwise.

**Phylogeny and origin counting.** We built a reference-guided multiple alignment of the concatenated *PAD1*+*FDC1* sequences, collapsed identical sequences into haplotypes, and built neighbor-joining (NJ) and UPGMA trees from *p*-distances with `Bio.Phylo` [@saitou1987]. NJ support came from 100 bootstrap replicates over alignment columns [@felsenstein1985]. We counted independent LOF origins in two ways. The tree-free lower bound is the number of distinct LOF mutations. The tree-based count is the minimum number of gains of each allele on the haplotype tree rooted on S288C, from Sankoff parsimony with an absent root state (our implementation, checked on toy trees).

**Statistics.** All tests are two-sided. The strain-level association between niche and predicted POF used Fisher's exact test (n = strains with a POF call). To account for relatedness, we stratified the Gallone strains by their published clade and used the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) test with continuity correction, the Mantel–Haenszel pooled odds ratio and its 95% confidence interval, and the Breslow–Day test of homogeneity [@mantel1959]. Only clades containing both domesticated (beer, saké, spirits) and reference (wine, wild, bioethanol) strains contribute. Genotype–phenotype agreement used Wilson 95% confidence intervals and Cohen's κ. Copy number was compared with Mann–Whitney *U* tests, effect sizes as Cliff's δ, Kruskal–Wallis tests across groups, and Benjamini–Hochberg correction across genes. Tests used SciPy and statsmodels [@virtanen2020; @seabold2010].

**Structure.** We parsed PDB 4ZAC chain A with `Bio.PDB`, listed residues within contact distance of prFMN and Mn^2+^ together with UniProt binding-site annotations, and asked which of them each truncated protein keeps. For display, the Cα trace was projected onto its first two principal axes.

**Software, environment and reproducibility.** Python 3.13.5 with Biopython 1.88, NumPy, pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, scikit-learn, matplotlib and seaborn [@cock2009; @harris2020; @mckinney2010; @virtanen2020; @seabold2010; @pedregosa2011; @hunter2007; @waskom2021]. Exact versions are pinned in `environment.yml`. The full analysis was run in Google Colab. One command (`python run_all.py`) runs every step, writes every figure and records every number quoted here in `results/results.json`. The manuscript is built from that file, so no number in the text was typed by hand. Comments at the top of each module state which lecture its technique comes from, or that it goes beyond the course (Fisher's exact test, CMH, Wilson intervals, Sankoff parsimony, Mann–Whitney *U*, Bio.PDB). No code was downloaded from third parties beyond the listed libraries.

(ii) The first version of the analysis notebook (data acquisition, variant calling, trees, structure and copy number) was generated by Claude in the claude.ai code-execution environment, following our proposal. (iii) Claude Code (model Claude Opus 5.5) refactored that notebook into the reproducible repository; implemented the relatedness-aware, phenotype-validation, origin-counting, 1,011-genome copy-number and lager-subgenome analyses; drew the figures; searched for and checked references against PubMed and DOI records; and drafted this manuscript. # 3. Results and Discussion

## Loss-of-function alleles of *PAD1* and *FDC1* are confined to domesticated lineages

Of the {{R['core_n_strains']}} strains, {{R['core_pof_calls']['POF-']}} were predicted POF−, {{R['core_pof_calls']['POF+']}} POF+ and {{R['core_pof_calls']['?']}} unresolved. We found {{len(R['a2_alleles'])}} distinct truncating mutations (Fig. 2). {{R['a2_n_distinct_lof']}} stop the protein early and were called LOF, and four of these recur: *FDC1* c.501dupA (p.K167fs; {{allele_n('c.501dupA')}} strains), *FDC1* c.460C>T (p.Q154\*; {{allele_n('c.460C>T')}}), *PAD1* c.305G>A (p.W102\*; {{allele_n('c.305G>A')}}) and *FDC1* c.160A>T (p.K54\*; {{allele_n('c.160A>T')}}). The rest are each found in a single strain. The {{R['a2_n_late_stops']}} late *FDC1* stops (W497\*, Y500\*) remove at most {{late_removed()}} of the protein and were not called LOF by the default rule.

Predicted POF− genotypes were strongly enriched in domesticated beverage strains (beer, ale, lager, saké, shochu and spirits): {{pct(R['core_fisher_domestic']['a_POFneg'], R['core_fisher_domestic']['a_POFneg']+R['core_fisher_domestic']['a_POFpos'])}}, compared with {{pct(R['core_fisher_domestic']['b_POFneg'], R['core_fisher_domestic']['b_POFneg']+R['core_fisher_domestic']['b_POFpos'])}} wild, wine and bioethanol strains (Fisher's exact test, odds ratio {{f1(R['core_fisher_domestic']['odds_ratio'])}}, {{P(R['core_fisher_domestic']['fisher_p'])}}; Fig. 3a). No wild or bioethanol strain carried a LOF allele, as Gallone *et al.* also reported [@gallone2016]. The same holds for beer against wild and wine strains alone ({{pct(R['core_fisher_beer']['a_POFneg'], R['core_fisher_beer']['a_POFneg']+R['core_fisher_beer']['a_POFpos'])}} vs {{pct(R['core_fisher_beer']['b_POFneg'], R['core_fisher_beer']['b_POFneg']+R['core_fisher_beer']['b_POFpos'])}}; {{P(R['core_fisher_beer']['fisher_p'])}}). The {{R['core_q154_in_pofneg_beer']['n']}} POF− Gallone beer strains are dominated by one *FDC1* allele: {{pct(R['core_q154_in_pofneg_beer']['k'], R['core_q154_in_pofneg_beer']['n'])}} carry Q154\*, close to the 73.8% that Mertens *et al.* reported for the narrower group of POF− Beer 1 strains [@mertens2019].

## The enrichment survives correction for relatedness

These strain-level *P* values overstate the evidence, because many beer strains are near-identical relatives and share one mutation by descent. We therefore grouped the {{R['a1_gallone_n_called']}} Gallone beverage, wine, wild and bioethanol strains with a POF call by their published clade [@gallone2016] and compared strains only within each group. A clade made up entirely of beer strains has nothing to compare against, so only {{R['a1_cmh_n_strata']}} clades ({{', '.join(R['a1_cmh_strata'])}}; {{R['a1_cmh_n_strains']}} strains) count. Even so, domesticated strains were more often POF− than wine, wild and bioethanol strains *within* the same clade (Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio {{f1(R['a1_cmh_or'])}}, 95% CI {{f1(R['a1_cmh_or_ci'][0])}}–{{f1(R['a1_cmh_or_ci'][1])}}; CMH {{P(R['a1_cmh_p'])}}; Fig. 3c). The odds ratios differed between clades (Breslow–Day {{P(R['a1_breslow_day_p'])}}): domesticated strains were more often POF− in {{R['a1_cmh_n_dom_higher']}} of the {{R['a1_cmh_n_contributing']}} clades that carry information, and Britain adds none because all its strains are POF−. The pooled estimate therefore rests on few strains, as its wide confidence interval shows. The Wine clade, which holds beer and spirits strains of wine ancestry, shows no enrichment. In other words, a wine-derived beer strain usually kept its wine-like *PAD1*–*FDC1*. At the clade level, {{R['a1_n_pofneg_clades']}} of {{R['a1_n_clades']}} clades are mostly POF− (Britain, US, Mixed and Asian), whereas Beer 2 and Wine are almost entirely POF+ (Fig. 3b).

## Clove flavor was lost at least {{R['a2_n_loss_events']}} separate times, through {{R['a2_n_distinct_lof']}} distinct mutations

Two different mutations cannot come from the same event, so the {{R['a2_n_distinct_lof']}} distinct LOF mutations arose separately. They are not all separate losses, though: a second stop codon in a gene that is already broken changes nothing. Joining mutations that ever occur in the same strain leaves {{R['a2_n_loss_events']}} groups that never share a strain, so POF was lost at least {{R['a2_n_loss_events']}} separate times, {{R['a2_n_beverage_loss_events']}} of them in beverage yeasts (Fig. 4). The ale haplotype (Q154\* + *PAD1* W102\*, almost always with c.501dupA downstream) defines a clade with {{f0(ale_clade()['support'])}}% bootstrap support that contains {{ale_clade()['n_strains']}} strains, all POF− and mostly from the Beer 1 clades (Britain, US, Belgium/Germany). Saké and shochu strains carry a different allele, K54\*, in a clade with {{f0(sake_clade()['support'])}}% support ({{sake_clade()['n_strains']}} strains, all POF−). On the haplotype tree K54\* maps to a single branch, as do *PAD1* W102\* and every private allele (minimum one gain each; Fig. 4). A second *PAD1* W102\* allele (c.306G>A, one Beer 2 strain) changes the neighboring base of the same codon, so it is a separate mutation, but that strain also carries K54\*, so it is not a separate loss. Further private losses occur in a wine strain (R309\*) and a laboratory strain (*PAD1* Y98\* with *FDC1* G288fs). The Beer 2 lineage carries its own late stop, W497\*, which Gallone *et al.* used as a marker to breed 4-VG− hybrids [@gallone2016].

Parsimony places Q154\* on {{allele_gain('c.460C>T')}} branches and c.501dupA on {{allele_gain('c.501dupA')}}. Bootstrap support in the backbone of the tree is low and yeast genomes recombine and form mosaics [@gallone2016; @peter2018], so we do not read these extra gains as recurrent mutation. The conservative conclusion is that POF was lost at least {{R['a2_n_loss_events']}} times: in Beer 1 ales, in Asian saké and shochu yeasts, and in single wine and laboratory strains. Beer 2 may add one more through the late stop W497\*, which our rule does not call LOF but which matches the measured phenotype (below). This fits Gallone *et al.*, who reconstructed a single LOF at the base of an early Beer 1 subclade and different mutations in other sublineages [@gallone2016].

## Every common truncation removes the cofactor, metal and active site of Fdc1

On the Fdc1 crystal structure (PDB 4ZAC), K54\*, Q154\* and K167fs all end the protein before every residue that binds prFMN or Mn^2+^ and before E285, the residue that carries out the reaction (Fig. 5a–c,e). K54\* keeps only {{pc(struct_keep('c.160A>T'))}} of the protein, not even the whole N-terminal UbiD domain; Q154\* and K167fs keep about a third, ending inside the prFMN-binding domain. *PAD1* W102\* and Y98\* remove {{struct_lost('PAD1','c.305G>A')}} of the {{struct_tot('PAD1','c.305G>A')}} annotated FMN- and DMAP-binding residues of the UbiX-like flavoprotein. These alleles therefore destroy the enzyme completely rather than weakening it. The private wine and laboratory alleles (R309\*, G288fs) are different. They keep {{struct_tot('FDC1','c.925A>T') - struct_lost('FDC1','c.925A>T')}} of the {{struct_tot('FDC1','c.925A>T')}} binding residues but delete the C-terminal UbiD domain, so their effect is less certain.

## Genotype predicts the measured phenotype, and the errors are consistent with hidden intact copies

Gallone *et al.* scored POF in the laboratory for {{R['a3_n_phenotyped']}} of their strains [@gallone2016]. For the {{R['a3_n_compared']}} strains that also had a genotype call, our prediction agreed with the phenotype in {{pc(R['a3_accuracy'][0])}} of cases (sensitivity {{pc(R['a3_sensitivity'][0])}}, 95% CI {{pc(R['a3_sensitivity'][1])}}–{{pc(R['a3_sensitivity'][2])}}; specificity {{pc(R['a3_specificity'][0])}}, {{pc(R['a3_specificity'][1])}}–{{pc(R['a3_specificity'][2])}}; Cohen's κ = {{f2(R['a3_kappa'])}}). The disagreements are not random (Fig. 3d). Strains carrying the full ale haplotype were POF− in {{sig('FDC1 K167fs + FDC1 Q154* + PAD1 W102*')}} cases, and K54\* strains in {{sig('FDC1 K54*')}}. In contrast, strains whose only truncating allele was c.501dupA were POF− in just {{sig('FDC1 K167fs')}}. This allele lies in a run of six adenines, which makes the call itself less certain. More importantly, one working copy is enough to make clove flavor, so a strain is POF− only if every copy of *FDC1* or *PAD1* is broken, and ale strains have a mean ploidy of about 3.5 [@mertens2019]. A consensus sequence that reports c.501dupA can hide an intact second allele, which would explain these false calls. The late stop W497\* behaves like a true LOF ({{sig('FDC1 W497*')}} POF−), consistent with its use as a breeding marker [@gallone2016]. Counting the late stops as LOF raises sensitivity to {{pc(R['a3_sensitivity_rules'][1]['sensitivity'])}}. Also ignoring a lone c.501dupA raises specificity to {{pc(R['a3_sensitivity_rules'][2]['specificity'])}}, at the cost of sensitivity ({{pc(R['a3_sensitivity_rules'][2]['sensitivity'])}}; Supplementary Table 3). Finally, {{sig('no truncating allele')}} strains without any truncating allele were nonetheless POF−. Their loss may lie elsewhere (missense changes, regulation or the gene copies themselves), or the phenotype call may be wrong.

## Maltose transporters show lineage-specific trade-offs, not a domestication-wide gain

Sugar use is the other trait that beer domestication is thought to have shaped [@vidgren2005; @gallone2016]. We estimated copy number from sequencing depth for {{R['a4_n_beer']}} beer and {{R['a4_n_wild']}} wild isolates in the 1,011-genome panel [@peter2018]. The total number of *MAL31*, *MAL11* and *MPH2/3* copies did not differ between beer and wild strains (mean {{f2(R['a4_ecology_beer_total_mean'])}} vs {{f2(R['a4_ecology_wild_total_mean'])}}; Mann–Whitney {{P(R['a4_beer_vs_wild_total_p'])}}; Cliff's δ = {{f2(R['a4_beer_vs_wild_total_cliffs_delta'])}}; Fig. 6a). This confirms, with {{f0((R['a4_n_beer']+R['a4_n_wild'])/(R['core_mal_assembly'][3]['domesticated_n']+R['core_mal_assembly'][3]['wild_lab_n']))}} times more strains, the null result from our assembly-based count. The individual genes, however, moved in opposite directions. Beer strains carry more *MAL31*-family copies ({{f2(R['a4_ecology_beer_mal31_mean'])}} vs {{f2(R['a4_ecology_wild_mal31_mean'])}}; Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted {{P(R['a4_beer_vs_wild_mal31_p_bh'])}}, δ = {{f2(R['a4_beer_vs_wild_mal31_cliffs_delta'])}}) and fewer *MPH2/3* copies ({{f2(R['a4_ecology_beer_mph2_3_mean'])}} vs {{f2(R['a4_ecology_wild_mph2_3_mean'])}}; adjusted {{P(R['a4_beer_vs_wild_mph2_3_p_bh'])}}, δ = {{f2(R['a4_beer_vs_wild_mph2_3_cliffs_delta'])}}). The Ale beer clade shows the strongest expansion (median total {{f2(R['a4_clade_ale_beer_total_median'])}} vs {{f2(R['a4_clade_wild_clades_total_median'])}} copies in wild clades; adjusted {{P(R['a4_ale_beer_clade_vs_wild_clades_total_p_bh'])}}; Fig. 6b), whereas the African beer clade has fewer copies than wild clades. Sugar-transporter changes therefore depend on the lineage, whereas POF loss always went the same way. No beer strain in this panel lacked *FDC1* altogether ({{R['a4_fdc1_fdc1_absent_beer_n']}} of {{R['a4_n_beer']}}), so these *S. cerevisiae* beer yeasts did not lose POF by deleting the gene; lager hybrids may differ (below).

## Comparison with earlier work

Our results agree with and extend the three studies that defined POF loss in beer yeast. Mukai *et al.* found a *PAD1* nonsense allele in ale yeast, *FDC1* nonsense alleles in saké, shochu and ale yeasts and an *FDC1* frameshift in lager yeast [@mukai2014]; we identify these as *PAD1* W102\*, *FDC1* Q154\*/K54\* and c.501dupA and show that they belong to distinct lineages. Gallone *et al.* described convergent, lineage-specific loss [@gallone2016]; we turn that observation into a count of {{R['a2_n_distinct_lof']}} distinct mutations and at least {{R['a2_n_loss_events']}} separate losses and show that the enrichment holds within clades. Mertens *et al.* reported Q154\* in 73.8% of POF− Beer 1 strains [@mertens2019]; our {{pc(R['core_q154_in_pofneg_beer']['k']/R['core_q154_in_pofneg_beer']['n'])}} across all POF− Gallone beers is consistent. New here are the genotype–phenotype check, which identifies c.501dupA calls as the main source of error, and the structural argument that every common allele is a complete null.

## Limitations

Our genotypes come from consensus sequences, which report one sequence per gene and can hide a second, intact copy. This matters because one working copy restores POF and many beer yeasts are polyploid [@mertens2019]. The predicted phenotype is therefore an upper bound on true loss for strains with a single truncating allele. We used a reference-guided alignment and distance trees; bootstrap support away from the main clades is low, so we based the origin count on distinct mutations rather than on the tree. The stratified test discards beer-only clades and so has limited power. The remaining strain-level *P* values ignore relatedness and are reported only for comparison. Some niche labels in the BLAST-derived panel were inferred from record titles, and four strains lack a *PAD1* record. For copy number, some genes share a column in the 1,011-genome matrix (*MAL31* with a paralogue, *MPH2* with *MPH3*), and the most divergent wild lineages may be under-counted by mapping to the reference. Only two lager genomes were available, so ale and lager could not be compared.

## From removing flavors to adding them

These strains show that brewers shaped flavor by taking something away. They knew nothing of Pad1 or Fdc1, but by reusing yeast from clean-tasting batches they kept strains with broken copies of these genes, again and again. Modern strain development runs the other way: it adds or tunes flavors. Genome surveys like this one help in both directions.

The non-GMO routes rely on natural variation. Screening hundreds of wild and industrial strains shows that aroma production varies by an order of magnitude, and breeding the best producers can yield hybrids that beat both parents [@steensels2014a; @steensels2014b]. Crossing *S. cerevisiae* with its wild relative *S. eubayanus* recreates lager-like strains and widens their aroma range [@krogerus2015; @krogerus2017; @mertens2015], and hybrids have repeatedly adapted to brewing niches [@gallone2019; @langdon2019]. Wild parents, however, bring back POF. The LOF mutations listed here are natural markers for removing it again, either by marker-assisted breeding with a natural allele such as *FDC1* W497\* [@gallone2016] or by introducing the natural Q154\* mutation into a hybrid, an approach Mertens *et al.* describe as cisgenic [@mertens2019].

Fruity thiols show the opposite case, where a common loss is reversed. Most *S. cerevisiae* strains carry an inactivating deletion in *IRC7*, a β-lyase that releases fruity volatile thiols from grape precursors [@roncoroni2011; @santiago2015]; a second β-lyase, Str3, can do the same [@holt2011]. *IRC7* is therefore another flavor gene broken across the species. Engineered brewing strains now add hop-like monoterpenes [@denby2018] or restore thiol release. Several engineered brewing strains have received FDA "no questions" letters in response to GRAS notices. Examples are Omega Yeast's OYR-243, which carries an extra copy of the yeast's own *IRC7* (GRN 1096), and Berkeley Yeast's BY-989, which carries a bacterial carbon–sulfur lyase (GRN 1094) [@fda_grn1096; @fda_grn1094]. One notice even mirrors our maltose axis in reverse: BY-1532 deletes maltose and maltotriose transport and metabolism genes to make non-alcoholic beer [@fda_grn1265]. A "no questions" letter records that the agency did not question the notifier's own safety conclusion; it is not an approval. Whether flavor is removed by selection or added by design, knowing which alleles control it in which lineages is the common starting point.

# 4. Conclusions

Across {{R['core_n_strains']}} strains, brewers' yeasts lost the clove-flavor pathway at least {{R['a2_n_loss_events']}} separate times, through {{R['a2_n_distinct_lof']}} distinct mutations. Every common mutation removes the part of Fdc1 that binds its cofactor and does the chemistry. The pattern holds in most groups of related strains, and our DNA-based calls predict laboratory phenotypes moderately well (κ = {{f2(R['a3_kappa'])}}). Maltose-transporter copy number, by contrast, changed in different ways in different lineages. POF loss is one of the clearest records, written in the genome, of people selecting a microbe for flavor.

**Future directions.**
**Allele-aware genotyping:** call heterozygous and polyploid genotypes from raw reads (SRA) to test whether masked intact alleles explain the c.501dupA discordance.
**Phenotype the edge cases:** measure 4-VG in the W497\* and lone-c.501dupA strains to settle their status.
**Maximum-likelihood phylogeny:** confirm the origin count with an ML tree and a progressive multiple alignment.
**Other flavor genes:** apply the same pipeline to *IRC7*, testing how widespread the inactivating deletion is in beer lineages.
**Lager subgenome:** extend the *S. eubayanus* analysis to more lager genomes.
